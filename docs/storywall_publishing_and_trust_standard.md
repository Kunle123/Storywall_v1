# Storywall Publishing and Trust Standard

**Author:** Manus AI  
**Date:** 2026-04-14

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the **minimum standard for what may be published on Storywall v1** and how trust should be made legible to readers. It is the operating policy that turns Storywall from a story-generation workflow into a credible publishing system.

Storywall is not intended to publish freeform opinion, thinly evidenced narratives, or visually polished but weakly grounded timelines. A Storywall may be interpretive, but it must remain **researchable, sourceable, and transparent about uncertainty**. The role of this standard is therefore to answer four questions clearly: what counts as sufficiently sourced, what counts as balanced enough to publish, what signals trust to the reader, and what should block publication outright.

| Standard principle | Meaning in practice |
|---|---|
| **Non-fiction only** | Publishable stories must be grounded in researchable facts |
| **Timeline mandatory** | Every story must preserve a chronological backbone |
| **References mandatory** | Stories must show visible evidence paths |
| **Interpretation separated** | Commentary must not be disguised as fact |
| **Disputes surfaced** | Contested topics must show alternate viewpoints where relevant |
| **Creator accountability** | The creator is visible as the responsible publisher |
| **Trust made legible** | Readers can understand support level, uncertainty, and source posture |
| **Simplicity over ceremony** | Trust should be clear without becoming noisy or overdesigned |

## 2. Scope

This standard applies to all Storywall v1 stories created through the AI-first hybrid workflow. It governs draft review, validation, and publication. It applies to story-level summaries, event cards, creator notes, synthesis sections, trust rails, and imagery use insofar as imagery can affect perceived factual certainty.

The standard does not attempt to define universal truth or adjudicate every historical disagreement. Its purpose is narrower and more practical: to define whether a Storywall draft is responsible and complete enough to publish under the Storywall product model.

| Covered area | Included in this standard |
|---|---|
| **Story framing** | Yes |
| **Timeline and event structure** | Yes |
| **Source visibility** | Yes |
| **Confidence and dispute states** | Yes |
| **Creator attribution** | Yes |
| **Subtle edit signaling** | Yes |
| **Imagery trust implications** | Yes |
| **Pure legal review** | No |
| **Platform moderation policy beyond story quality** | No |

## 3. Publishing Philosophy

A Storywall should earn trust through visible discipline rather than through branding claims. The product should not ask readers to trust the platform abstractly. Instead, it should show them what the story is, who made it, what evidence supports it, where uncertainty exists, and where interpretation begins.

This means the publication standard must reject two opposite failure modes. On one side, Storywall must reject under-sourced and editorialized stories that overstate certainty. On the other side, it must also reject bland timelines that merely list dates without context, synthesis, or a clear explanatory frame. A publishable Storywall is both **grounded** and **useful**.

| Failure mode | Why it fails Storywall |
|---|---|
| **Unsourced interpretation** | Makes claims without a visible evidence path |
| **Thin chronology** | Provides too little depth to justify publication |
| **One-sided disputed framing** | Treats contested interpretation as settled fact |
| **Decorative sourcing** | Shows references that do not materially support the claims |
| **Image-led distortion** | Lets visuals imply more certainty than the evidence supports |
| **Context-free event listing** | Does not add meaning beyond a basic timeline |

## 4. Core Publish Requirements

Every publishable Storywall must satisfy a minimum structural standard before any story-specific nuances are considered. These are the baseline conditions for publication.

| Requirement | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Explicit story frame** | Subject, angle, and scope are visible to the reader |
| **Coherent timeline** | Events are ordered or grouped in a meaningful chronological sequence |
| **Visible references** | Readers can inspect the supporting evidence path |
| **Factual summaries** | Event summaries remain factual rather than editorialized |
| **Creator identity** | The responsible creator is visible |
| **Trust cues** | Confidence, dispute, or support posture is visible where needed |
| **Contextual adequacy** | The story is broad enough to provide meaningful understanding |
| **Ending synthesis** | The story concludes with a sense-making block, not abrupt chronology |

If any of these are missing, the story should not be publishable.

## 5. Source Sufficiency Standard

A Storywall story must be more than merely “cited.” It must be **sufficiently sourced**. Source sufficiency means that the evidence attached to the story is meaningful, relevant to the claims being made, and adequate for the scale of the framing.

The system should evaluate sufficiency at both the **story level** and the **event level**. A story can fail either one. A richly cited hero section cannot compensate for unsupported events, and a collection of sourced event cards cannot compensate for a story frame that overclaims beyond the evidence available.

| Level | Minimum standard |
|---|---|
| **Story level** | The overall framing, summary, and synthesis must be supportable by the evidence profile of the story |
| **Event level** | Each material event must have at least one meaningful evidence path and an appropriate confidence state |
| **Claim level** | Strong causal or disputed claims require stronger support than neutral descriptive claims |

### 5.1 Story-level sufficiency

A story is sufficiently sourced at the story level when a reasonable reader can understand the basis of the story’s framing, chronology, and main synthesis through the references provided. Story-level sufficiency is not a simple source count threshold. It is a relevance and coverage threshold.

| Story-level check | Publish expectation |
|---|---|
| **Coverage across the stated scope** | The evidence should span the major periods or phases of the story |
| **Framing support** | The selected angle should be plausible based on the events and sources included |
| **Synthesis support** | The concluding synthesis should not introduce unsupported claims |
| **Trust transparency** | Any weak or disputed portions should be visible rather than hidden |

### 5.2 Event-level sufficiency

Each material event in the timeline should have an evidence path appropriate to its role in the story. Routine descriptive events may need modest support. Turning points, allegations, or causal claims require stronger support and greater caution.

| Event type | Minimum support expectation |
|---|---|
| **Standard event** | At least one relevant source or evidence path |
| **Turning point** | Stronger corroboration or clear explanation of uncertainty |
| **Context note** | Support for the contextual claim it introduces |
| **Synthesis event/block** | Must be grounded in the accumulated story evidence rather than new unsupported claims |
| **Chatter / low-confidence development** | May appear only if explicitly labeled and visually de-emphasized |

### 5.3 What counts as a meaningful evidence path

Not every linked item is meaningful support. Decorative sourcing should not satisfy the standard. A source should be treated as meaningful only if it materially supports, documents, contextualizes, or evidences the event or claim to which it is attached.

| Source relationship | Counts toward sufficiency? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Directly documents the event** | Yes | Strong support |
| **Corroborates the event from a separate source** | Yes | Strengthens confidence |
| **Provides necessary context for interpretation** | Yes, with care | Useful if clearly contextual rather than evidentiary |
| **Mentions the subject only incidentally** | No | Too weak |
| **Is attached only because it shares a keyword** | No | Decorative, not relevant |
| **Supports only a broader theme but not the specific claim** | Sometimes | Must not be used to overstate certainty |

## 6. Source Quality and Weighting

The Storywall standard should not force a single source hierarchy for every story type, but it should distinguish between stronger and weaker evidence classes. Source quality affects confidence state, dispute handling, and publish review.

| Source class | Typical role in Storywall |
|---|---|
| **Primary records / archives** | Strong evidence for factual anchors |
| **Official statements / documents** | Useful for dates, actions, and positions |
| **Established reporting** | Common support for public developments |
| **Interviews / firsthand accounts** | Valuable but may require contextual caution |
| **Analytical commentary** | Useful for interpretation, not direct proof of underlying facts |
| **Social posts / informal reports** | Weak support unless corroborated |
| **Unverified online chatter** | Not sufficient for standard factual claims |

The system should use source class as one input into trust posture, but not as a substitute for actual relevance. A prestigious source attached irrelevantly should not count more than a relevant source attached correctly.

## 7. Confidence-State Standard

Every event and, where useful, every story should carry an internal confidence assessment. Some of this may be shown directly to readers; some may be used primarily for validation and moderation. Confidence labels help Storywall remain honest about what is known, what is inferred, and what remains unsettled.

| Confidence state | Meaning | Publish treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Verified** | Strongly supported and stable | Freely publishable |
| **Mostly verified** | Well supported with minor uncertainty | Publishable |
| **Emerging** | Plausible but still unstable or weakly supported | Publishable only with clear caution |
| **Disputed** | Material sources or interpretations conflict | Publishable only if alternate views are surfaced |
| **Unsupported** | Current evidence does not support the claim adequately | Not publishable |
| **Chatter** | Reported but low-confidence development | May appear only if clearly labeled and de-emphasized |

### 7.1 Chatter policy

The `chatter` category exists to prevent the product from flattening all uncertain reports into either “fact” or “nothing.” In some stories, especially around recent or unstable developments, low-confidence reports may be part of the public narrative. Storywall may include them, but only as low-weight, clearly marked elements that do not carry the same editorial weight as verified events.

| Chatter rule | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Labeling** | Must be visibly marked as low-confidence or equivalent |
| **Visual weight** | Must not resemble a major verified turning point |
| **Causal claims** | Should not anchor strong causal interpretation |
| **Imagery** | Should generally remain text-only |
| **Publication** | Allowed only when inclusion improves reader understanding of uncertainty |

## 8. Disputed Claims and Alternate Viewpoints

Storywall v1 should avoid pretending that every important topic has one uncontested reading. At the same time, it should not collapse into false balance. The standard should therefore require alternate-view surfacing when disagreement is materially relevant to the reader’s understanding of the story.

A story is not required to present every fringe interpretation. It is required to surface **meaningful, evidence-backed disagreement** when the chosen frame touches disputed ground.

| Situation | Required treatment |
|---|---|
| **Simple factual event with broad agreement** | No alternate-view module needed |
| **Interpretive disagreement with material significance** | Surface alternate viewpoints or dispute note |
| **Allegation or contested causality** | Separate what is established from what is contested |
| **Public memory / legacy dispute** | Show that multiple readings exist if central to the story angle |
| **Fringe or unsupported theory** | Do not elevate merely for symmetry |

### 8.1 Dispute-handling rules

| Rule | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Separate factual baseline from interpretation** | Prevents disputed readings from presenting as settled fact |
| **Show alternate views where they affect the story’s meaning** | Preserves reader trust |
| **Do not over-equalize fringe views** | Avoids false balance |
| **Mark uncertainty close to the relevant claim** | Keeps trust cues usable rather than abstract |
| **Require stronger review for contested causal framing** | These are the highest-risk editorial areas |

## 9. Separation of Fact and Commentary

One of the central Storywall trust rules is that factual summary and creator interpretation must not silently blend together. The creator may absolutely shape meaning, but the reader should be able to recognize which parts are event summary and which parts are commentary or synthesis.

| Content layer | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Event summary** | Factual, restrained, and source-aware |
| **Creator note** | Explicitly interpretive or reflective |
| **Story framing statement** | Clearly marked as the story’s angle rather than neutral fact |
| **Synthesis** | May be interpretive, but must remain grounded in the story evidence |

The product should preserve this distinction in data structure, interface design, and validation. A story that repeatedly inserts interpretation into factual fields should fail validation even if the underlying sources are strong.

## 10. Creator Attribution and Accountability

A key trust signal in Storywall is that the reader can see who created the story. Trust is not only about sources; it is also about identifiable editorial responsibility.

| Attribution requirement | Publish standard |
|---|---|
| **Creator identity visible** | Required |
| **Creator role clear** | Required if multiple contributors exist later |
| **Story ownership traceable internally** | Required |
| **Anonymous publication by default** | Not recommended for v1 |

The interface should make creator attribution easy to understand and should not bury it beneath interactions. The reader should not have to search for the publisher of the story.

## 11. Edit Signaling Standard

The user requirement is that edit signaling should remain subtle. Storywall should therefore avoid loud authorship theatrics while still preserving a meaningful trust posture.

The preferred model is to retain full internal provenance while exposing only a light-touch reader signal when manual revision materially shapes the final story. In most cases, trust should be communicated primarily through sources, creator identity, and dispute handling rather than through aggressive “AI wrote this / human edited this” labeling.

| Edit-signaling layer | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Internal provenance** | Full revision and generation history retained |
| **Reader-facing default** | No noisy line-by-line authorship labeling |
| **Substantial creator revision** | Optional subtle “creator revised” treatment |
| **Misleading concealment of material changes** | Not permitted internally, even if reader UI remains subtle |

## 12. Scope Adequacy Standard

A story may be factually sourced and still fail Storywall if it is too brief, too narrow, or too slight for its stated frame. The product should therefore evaluate **scope adequacy** as part of trust and publish review. This is important because a thin story can mislead by omission even when the facts it includes are individually correct.

| Scope risk | Meaning | Publish effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Too narrow for frame** | Angle promises broad understanding but covers only a tiny slice | Block or require reframing |
| **Too brief** | Story lacks enough depth to deliver useful context | Block or warn depending on severity |
| **Missing key period** | Important phase omitted in a way that distorts meaning | Block if materially misleading |
| **Overloaded with trivial events** | Quantity substitutes for meaningful context | Warn or require compression |

This standard directly supports the user’s definition of a bad Storywall: something too brief, too narrow in timeslice, or excessively editorialized.

## 13. Imagery and Trust

Imagery affects trust because visuals can imply confidence, importance, and factuality. Storywall should therefore treat imagery as part of the publishing standard, not merely as decoration.

| Imagery trust rule | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Hero imagery** | Usually allowed if it supports the story tone and does not mislead |
| **Turning-point imagery** | Allowed selectively for major events only |
| **Context / synthesis imagery** | Allowed when it clarifies the story |
| **Standard event-card imagery** | Usually disallowed |
| **Low-confidence event imagery** | Generally disallowed |
| **Recognizable person generation** | Prefer non-photorealistic editorial treatment |

### 13.1 Imagery misuse conditions

| Misuse | Why it is a trust problem |
|---|---|
| **Image attached to low-confidence event as if established** | Overstates certainty |
| **Photorealistic generated depiction implying documentary authenticity** | Risks misleading readers |
| **Arbitrary image repetition** | Weakens editorial credibility |
| **Image chosen for drama rather than relevance** | Distorts reader perception |

## 14. Validation Outcomes

The publishing system should convert trust review into clear outcome states. Validation should not merely tell creators that something is wrong; it should classify the severity of the issue and the effect on publication.

| Validation result | Meaning | Publication status |
|---|---|---|
| **Pass** | Story meets the Storywall standard | Publish allowed |
| **Pass with warnings** | Story is publishable but quality risks remain visible | Publish allowed after acknowledgment |
| **Blocked** | Story fails one or more mandatory publish conditions | Publish not allowed |

### 14.1 Warning conditions

| Warning condition | Typical reason |
|---|---|
| **Minor source thinness in non-central areas** | Some peripheral events are less robustly supported |
| **Limited but visible dispute handling** | Acceptable but could be clearer |
| **Scope slightly tighter than ideal** | Story works but might benefit from broader context |
| **Emerging developments included with caution** | Publishable if clearly labeled |

### 14.2 Block conditions

| Block condition | Reason |
|---|---|
| **No visible references** | Mandatory trust requirement not met |
| **No coherent timeline** | Core Storywall structure missing |
| **Unsupported key claims** | Reader would be misled about evidence strength |
| **Contested framing presented as settled fact** | Major trust failure |
| **Insufficient breadth for stated frame** | Story materially underdelivers on its promise |
| **Fact/commentary blending in core fields** | Reader cannot distinguish evidence from interpretation |
| **Imagery misuse that overstates certainty** | Visual presentation undermines trust |

## 15. Review Workflow Standard

The publishing standard should inform how review happens operationally. Trust review should not be a vague editorial pass. It should be a structured check against explicit criteria.

| Review step | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Structural check** | Confirm title, framing, timeline, synthesis, creator identity, and references exist |
| **Evidence check** | Confirm major events and claims have meaningful support |
| **Dispute check** | Confirm contested issues are surfaced where needed |
| **Tone check** | Confirm summaries remain factual and restrained |
| **Scope check** | Confirm the story is broad enough and not misleading by omission |
| **Imagery check** | Confirm images follow policy and do not distort certainty |

Where possible, these checks should be partly automated and partly reviewable by the creator before publication. High-risk stories may additionally require a reviewer layer.

## 16. Reader-Facing Trust Presentation Standard

Trust cues should be visible, but they should not overwhelm the reading experience. Storywall is an editorial product, not a compliance dashboard. The standard should therefore require a compact trust presentation that is legible on mobile and consistent across stories.

| Reader-facing trust element | What it should communicate |
|---|---|
| **Creator label** | Who made the story |
| **Reference access** | Where the evidence comes from |
| **Confidence / dispute note** | Whether sections are stable, emerging, or contested |
| **Creator note styling** | Which parts are interpretive |
| **Subtle revision cue** | Whether the final story materially reflects creator revision |

The reader should be able to understand the trust posture without opening multiple hidden settings. The trust layer should be present, but quiet.

## 17. Operational Acceptance Criteria

The Storywall publishing and trust system should be considered correctly implemented only if the following conditions are met.

| ID | Acceptance criterion |
|---|---|
| **PTS-1** | A story cannot be published without visible references |
| **PTS-2** | A story cannot be published without a coherent timeline backbone |
| **PTS-3** | Unsupported key claims trigger a block rather than a warning |
| **PTS-4** | Disputed material claims trigger alternate-view handling or an equivalent dispute cue |
| **PTS-5** | Fact and commentary are stored and presented as distinct layers |
| **PTS-6** | Low-confidence chatter is visibly de-emphasized and cannot masquerade as a major verified event |
| **PTS-7** | Creator attribution is visible on all published stories |
| **PTS-8** | Image policy enforcement prevents misleading or overconfident visual presentation |
| **PTS-9** | Thin, overly narrow, or under-contextualized stories can be blocked for inadequacy even if some sources exist |
| **PTS-10** | The reader can understand the story’s trust posture without leaving the story surface |

## 18. Final Standard

A publishable Storywall is not merely a generated draft with citations attached. It is a **creator-approved, timeline-based non-fiction story** that is broad enough to be useful, sourced enough to be trustworthy, disciplined enough to separate fact from interpretation, and transparent enough to surface uncertainty where uncertainty matters.

That is the Storywall v1 publishing bar. Anything materially below it should remain in review rather than be published.
